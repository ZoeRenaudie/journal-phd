# **Wikidata exhibitions are best modeled as event-centered, linked records, but current practice still underspecifies curatorial complexity**

## 1. Introduction

Wikidata exhibition modeling works best when an exhibition is treated as a linked cultural event connected to artworks, people, places, and times, rather than as an isolated catalog record. That event-centered view aligns with the dominant cultural-heritage modeling literature, where CIDOC CRM and related approaches tie objects, actors, places, and times together through events, and where linked data is valued for interoperability, reuse, and cross-institutional aggregation  (Ferreira-Lopes and González-Gracia 2025, 1 - 16; Silva and Terra 2023, 93 - 107; Ranjgar et al. 2022; Doerr 2003, 75-92; Bruseker et al. 2017, 93-131). In the exhibition-specific literature, this same logic is applied to exhibition histories, virtual exhibitions, and curatorial data, but with added pressure to represent artworks shown together, venue changes, curatorial narratives, and public responses  (Wildenhaus 2019, 22 - 34; Llewellyn et al. 2025, 188-; Monaco et al. 2022; Rodwell and Whitelaw 2024, 161 - 187).

The literature also shows a tension between Wikidata’s practical strengths and its conceptual limits. Wikidata is widely useful as a multilingual authority hub and linking layer for cultural entities, and museums use it to enrich discovery and connect identifiers across collections  (Fagerving 2023; Sohmen et al. 2026; Zhu et al. 2023, 20 - 46). But exhibition scholars repeatedly argue that standard linked-data patterns often flatten distinctions between an exhibition concept, its individual stagings, its discourse, and its documentation, especially for touring or reconstructed exhibitions  (Rodríguez-Ortega 2024).

### 3.2 Exhibition Entity Design

The strongest exhibition-specific finding is that modeling improves when exhibitions are split into a conceptual layer and an occurrence layer. OntoExhibit separates an “Exhibition-Curatorial Project” from each realized “Exhibition,” making the staging both an event and a manifestation of a curatorial concept  (Rodríguez-Ortega 2024). That distinction matters for traveling exhibitions, because each stop can differ in layout, loans, and narrative emphasis even when they share a common project  (Rodríguez-Ortega 2024).

More general ontology work points the same way. Event-centric models are repeatedly preferred when institutions need to relate actors, places, dates, and objects precisely, especially when objects move or acquire new contextual roles over time  (Stein and Balandi 2019; Dijkshoorn et al. 2018, 255 - 271; Silva and Terra 2023, 93 - 107). In practice, this means Wikidata exhibition items are most defensible when they function as event nodes and carry links outward to participants, venues, dates, and exhibited objects, rather than trying to collapse all semantics into object records alone  (Manessi and Vazaiou 2024; Koch et al. 2024, 396-412).

The main difference is not whether exhibitions can be represented, but how much semantics can be carried natively. Wikidata is strong for identifiers, reconciliation, and cross-dataset links, while CIDOC CRM-style models are stronger for explicit event logic, provenance, and role-rich interpretation  (Freire and Isaac 2019, 59-68; Untner 2025; Kesäniemi et al. 2022, 542-549).

### 3.4 Implementation and Workflow

Implementation papers converge on a practical workflow: reconcile entities, create stable identifiers, model relationships conservatively, then enrich or export into a richer ontology when needed. Museums and archives repeatedly use Wikidata or Wikibase as a hub for authority control, enrichment, and federation, especially for people, places, and events  (Fagerving 2023; Sohmen et al. 2026; Zhu et al. 2023, 20 - 46). Several studies also show that Wikibase can serve as an editable front end for non-experts while exporting CIDOC CRM-compliant RDF through conventions or mappings  (Kesäniemi et al. 2022, 542-549).

This workflow is attractive because full ontology mapping is labor-intensive and often demands advanced technical literacy  (Stein and Balandi 2019; Ferreira-Lopes and González-Gracia 2025, 1 - 16). The tradeoff is that simpler graphs are easier to maintain but risk losing distinctions that matter for exhibition studies, such as multiple interpretations, chronology structure, and curatorial reformulations  (Van Ruymbeke et al. 2018; Voronova and Adamou 2026).

## 4. Discussion

The evidence supports a moderate-confidence conclusion: Wikidata is useful for exhibition modeling as a practical linked-data layer, but weak as a complete semantic model of exhibition studies. Its strengths are persistent identifiers, multilingual reconciliation, authority linking, and low-barrier collaborative editing  (Manessi and Vazaiou 2024; Sohmen et al. 2026). Those strengths are especially valuable where institutions need to connect distributed data and expose it for visualization, discovery, or reuse  (Fagerving 2023; Chen 2023, 700-721).

The main limitation is representational depth. Several papers show that cultural-heritage semantics often exceed what a single generic model captures, leading projects to extend CIDOC CRM, combine ontologies, or distinguish scholarly interpretations from asserted facts  (Daquino et al. 2016, 1 - 21; Untner 2025). Exhibition-specific work pushes this further by arguing that curatorial discourse, reenactments, and non-human agency require explicit classes and relations that current general-purpose platforms rarely expose directly  (Rodríguez-Ortega 2024).

Data quality and provenance remain persistent constraints. Wikidata can express substantial semantics, but studies evaluating its reuse in cultural heritage still report the need for human interpretation, validation, and provenance-aware workflows  (Freire and Isaac 2019, 59-68; Sohmen et al. 2026; Candela 2023, 866 - 878). This is particularly important for exhibitions because the same show can be documented differently across catalogues, venues, and retrospective scholarship  (Llewellyn et al. 2025, 188-; Daquino et al. 2016, 1 - 21; Chen et al. 2026).

## 5. Conclusion

The literature converges on a practical answer: use Wikidata to model exhibitions as linked events and as a reconciliation hub, but do not expect it to encode the full intellectual structure of exhibition history without additional conventions or ontology mappings. The strongest evidence supports event-centered, identifier-rich, interoperable modeling, while the weakest area is formal representation of curatorial discourse, touring variants, and interpretive uncertainty  (Rodríguez-Ortega 2024; Freire and Isaac 2019, 59-68; Voronova and Adamou 2026).

### Research Gaps

The biggest gaps appear where exhibition scholarship demands more than basic linked-data interoperability. Most papers focus on infrastructure, mappings, or adjacent museum data, while only a few model exhibitions themselves as complex curatorial and discursive objects  (Rodríguez-Ortega 2024).

The sharpest gap is direct empirical work on Wikidata-native exhibition modeling. Exhibition-specific papers mostly discuss linked data conceptually or develop external ontologies, while practical Wikidata studies emphasize enrichment, authority control, and visualization rather than exhibition semantics themselves  (Wildenhaus 2019, 22 - 34; Fagerving 2023; Kesäniemi et al. 2022, 542-549). A second gap is controlled vocabulary for curatorial structures and display logic, which OntoExhibit identifies as essential but still missing  (Rodríguez-Ortega 2024). A third gap is robust provenance for exhibition-level assertions, especially where records are reconstructed from catalogues, social media, or historical documentation  (Llewellyn et al. 2025, 188-; Guillem et al. 2025; Theodoridou et al. 2010, 169-210).

### Open Research Questions

Future work is likely to matter most where Wikidata, Wikibase, and richer heritage ontologies meet. The field needs tested patterns, not only conceptual proposals.

| Question | Why |
|---|---|
| **How should Wikidata distinguish an exhibition concept from each venue-specific staging?** | Touring and reconstructed shows change across venues, but current lightweight practice often flattens those differences. |
| **Which minimal property set best captures curatorial narrative, displayed works, and participant roles in Wikidata?** | Exhibition semantics remain understandardized, limiting consistency, queryability, and cross-project reuse. |
| **How can provenance and uncertainty for exhibition assertions be validated at scale across Wikidata and external graphs?** | Reconciliation is useful but still error-prone, and exhibition data are often reconstructed from heterogeneous historical sources. |
